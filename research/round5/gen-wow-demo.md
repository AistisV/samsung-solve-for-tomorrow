# Round 5 generator: WOW-first ("what is the 2-minute stage moment?")

**Lens.** Start from the moment on stage when the jury leans forward, laughs or gasps. Then check that a real person would use the thing again and again. Most of these ideas rest on one trick: **the device hears or feels something that people can't**, through contact microphones, vibration ML, acoustic localisation, ultrasound, radar or the mains hum. You can play that live on stage, and it doesn't look like a CO₂ light or a points app.

**Budget.** 4 WebSearch calls, all spent checking prior art for the top picks. Everything else comes from my own knowledge and is tagged **[unverified]**. I have not invented any statistics. Where a number would help, the profile says what to look up.

**Scores.** Each filter is scored 0–2: **WANT** · **WOW** · **NOT-SEEN** · **BUILD** (6 weeks, working demo) · **AI** (does real work) · **BIG** (scales) · **LT** (hook). Total out of 14. Theme: **S** = sustainability, **Sp** = sport.

## 2. Top 5 profiles

### #1 Ledo ausis ("Ice Ear"): the smart ice pick that hears thin ice (13/14)

- **What it physically is.** Nordic tour skaters already carry an ice pole (the Swedish *ispik*). They tap the ice ahead of them and **judge its thickness by ear**, a skill that takes years to learn [unverified: from general knowledge of Nordic ice-skating practice]. Our version is a 3D-printed ice-pick handle with an ESP32, a piezo contact disc and a MEMS mic, linked to a phone over Bluetooth. A phone-only mode (tap with any metal pick near the phone) makes the pick optional.
- **Demo script.** Three shallow trays of ice sit on stage, frozen at home to 3, 6 and 10 cm and kept in cool boxes.
  1. "People in Sweden have listened to ice for a hundred years. We taught a phone to do it."
  2. Tap each tray. The phone says the estimated thickness and speaks a verdict (red, amber or green).
  3. Play a recording of the famous "Star Wars pew-pew" sound of thin lake ice and explain that the chirp *is* the thickness measurement.
  4. Show the shared ice map of Lithuanian lakes: green and red dots, dated, from every angler who tapped.
- **What the AI actually does.** Flexural waves in floating ice are dispersive: high and low frequencies travel at different speeds, and that relationship depends on thickness. A microphone recording of an impulse (a tap, a jump or a skate) can recover thickness **within 5–10% of drilled-hole measurements** [verified: Cold Regions Sci. & Tech. 2023, "Microphone recording of flexural waves for estimation of lake ice thickness"]. The team would do two things:
  - use that physics to fit thickness from the chirp;
  - train a small classifier on the spectrogram to flag bad ice that thickness alone misses: slush, layered "white" ice or cracks.

  The map then mixes crowdsourced readings over time into a per-lake picture that fades as the readings get older.
- **Who uses it repeatedly, and how it scales.** Ice fishers test the ice on every trip, often every weekend all winter. Skaters, rescue services (PAGD) and municipalities with popular lakes could use the map. It scales to every cold country (Nordics, the Baltics, Canada, the northern US).
- **Closest existing thing.** **IceSound** (ACM IMWUT 2025) turns a smartphone into an ice-thickness sensor by playing a 3–22 kHz chirp through its speaker [verified: dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3749543]. It is an academic prototype, not a product in anglers' hands [unverified]. There are also handheld radar ice-thickness units, which are expensive [unverified]. **How ours differs:**
  - it uses the tap people already make with their ice pick, not the phone's speaker;
  - it adds the crowdsourced lake map;
  - it adds the "bad ice" classifier.

  The team should cite IceSound openly as validation that the method works.
- **LT hook.** Winter ice fishing is a mass hobby. Every winter the news covers anglers rescued from drifting ice floes on the Curonian Lagoon, and ice drownings [unverified: look up PAGD winter statistics before using this on a slide].
- **BOM.** ESP32-S3 (€10), piezo disc and preamp (€5), I2S MEMS mic (€5), 3D-printed handle, a steel tip taken from an existing ice pick (€10). About **€30**.
- **Biggest risk.** Data. Real lake ice only arrives in about late December, after the Nov 6 submission. The early prototype has to use freezer trays or a frozen kiddie pool outside in November. Tray ice sitting on a table does not flex like ice floating on water, so the trays would need to float in water tubs. The honest pitch is "validated on tank ice; lake pilot in January".

### #2 Vamzdžio ausis ("Pipe Ear"): one clip that names every drop in your flat (13/14)

- **What it physically is.** ShowerCoach was the one round-4 idea the team *wanted* ("I'd lowk have it in my bath"). This makes it bigger than a gadget. It is a clip-on puck with an ESP32, an accelerometer or piezo and a temperature probe, strapped to the apartment's cold and hot water pipes near the meters, with no plumber needed. Two optional extras:
  - a small e-ink or LED "shower buddy" in the bathroom (the ShowerCoach part);
  - an old Galaxy phone as the home hub.
- **Demo script.** A rig of one pipe with a tap, a toilet cistern and a shower head, all fed from a pressurised bucket or a garden pump.
  1. Open the tap. The app shows "kitchen tap, cold".
  2. Flush. The app shows "toilet, ~6 L".
  3. Leave the cistern leaking. The app says "silent toilet leak: X L/day ≈ €Y/year" and the jury leans forward.
  4. Finish on the household dashboard: "your shower = 41% of your hot-water bill".
- **What the AI actually does.**
  - *Non-intrusive disaggregation.* Each fixture has a vibration and flow "fingerprint": how the valve opens, the flow noise and how long it runs. A tiny CNN on the ESP32 or phone classifies the events. A calibration week against the apartment's own meter readings turns event duration into litres.
  - *Night-time anomaly detection* for trickles and drips.

  Research says this is feasible but not solved: about **70–80% accuracy for toilet and shower**, lower for handwashing [verified: Sensors 2025, "Noninvasive Acoustic Recognition of Water Flow Sources…", doi 10.3390/s25196221]. That leaves real work for the team without being hopeless.
- **Who uses it repeatedly, and how it scales.** The household sees it every day (the bathroom display is what hooks the dad). Block administrators and housing associations (bendrijos) can find the silent leaks that drive up shared-water charges. Water utilities are the long-term payer. It works in any block of flats in Europe.
- **Closest existing thing.**
  - Flume and Phyn read the main meter or sit inline, and they are US-centric [unverified].
  - LeakBot (UK) is a clip-on that detects leaks from pipe temperature, but it doesn't identify fixtures [unverified].
  - Academic prototypes exist (see above).

  **How ours differs:** it is a €20 clip-on for Soviet-era flats that are already sub-metered, it recognises individual fixtures, and it includes a shower-side "live € meter" that kids and parents actually look at.
- **LT hook.** Most Lithuanians live in Soviet-era blocks with individual hot and cold water meters. Hot water is billed through the heat price, and residents argue about the circulation fee ("gyvatukas") and shared-water discrepancies [unverified: confirm current Vilnius hot-water €/m³ from the Vilniaus šilumos tinklai tariff].
- **BOM.** ESP32-C3 (€4), ADXL355 or cheap piezo and preamp (€5–25), 2× DS18B20 (€3), a 3D-printed clamp, and optionally a 2.9" e-ink display (€15). About **€25–50**.
- **Biggest risk.** Fixtures in *neighbouring* flats vibrate the same shared riser pipe. Mounting on the apartment's own branch pipe after the meter, and using a temperature signature, reduces this, but the team has to test it in a real block in week 2. The WOW is gentler than the ice or beetle ideas; the "silent toilet leak" reveal carries it.

### #3 Court Ear: the solar box that turns any outdoor hoop into a stats court (12/14)

- **What it physically is.** A weatherproof box clamped behind the backboard, with an ESP32-S3, a contact mic on the rim bracket, an air mic, a small solar panel and a LoRa or WiFi link. It has **no camera**, so it raises no privacy issues and works in the dark: in Lithuania's winter, school-time daylight is short. Players see their stats on their phones via a QR code on the pole.
- **Demo script.** A portable hoop stands on stage.
  1. A student shoots five. Live calls appear: "swish", "rim-in", "clank", "airball (no contact)".
  2. Invite a juror to shoot. The jury laughs when the airball gets called out.
  3. Show a municipality dashboard: "Court on X street: 312 shots on Tuesdays, 0 after 18:00 in winter, so it needs a light, not a new court."
- **What the AI actually does.** A small audio and vibration classifier works through several steps:
  - it separates rim hits, backboard hits and net swishes (the contact mic captures rim vibration);
  - it counts events and estimates the make rate;
  - it runs session detection, so it can tell a 1-on-1 game from solo shooting;
  - it adds up occupancy over time.

  This is audio-event classification on hard, noisy real-world data (dribbling, wind, voices), not a wrapper.
- **Who uses it repeatedly, and how it scales.** Teens use it every evening: "court king" leaderboards, streaks, challenges between neighbourhood courts. Municipalities and school owners get the first real usage data on the courts they fund, which tells them where to add lighting, repairs or 3x3 events. It scales to every public court.
- **Closest existing thing.** Vision apps (HomeCourt) and open-source YOLO shot trackers need a phone on a tripod and light [verified that vision trackers exist: github.com/avishah3/AI-Basketball-Shot-Detection-Tracker]. Patents exist for sensor-based make/miss on the hoop or net [verified: USPTO 10159884 "Basketball make-miss shot sensing"]. As far as I know, no permanent, solar, camera-free court box is aimed at public-court usage data [unverified].
- **LT hook.** Basketball is the national religion, and every block yard has a hoop. "Krepšinio aikštelės" funded through participatory budgets could get usage data [unverified: check whether Vilnius or Kaunas participatory budgets have funded courts].
- **BOM.** ESP32-S3 (€10), piezo and mic (€8), 5 W solar panel with an 18650 and charger (€20), enclosure and clamp (€10), optional LoRa (€10). About **€50–60 per hoop**.
- **Biggest risk.** Theft and vandalism on public courts, and accuracy: a soft rim-in versus a swish off the net. The pilot would be one school court plus one yard court, measured against a hand-kept tally. The theme is sport, which the team leans away from, but it is clearly not a points-and-steps app.

### #4 Kinivarpų stetoskopas: hear the bark beetle before the forest turns red (12/14)

- **What it physically is.** A handheld "tree stethoscope": a contact piezo or accelerometer on a spike or a strap pressed to the bark, an ESP32 or phone ADC, and headphones plus an app. For season-long monitoring there is a strap-on logger version (ESP32, LoRa) that sits on high-risk trees at the edge of a stand. Beetles attack stand edges first [unverified].
- **Demo script.**
  1. Put two spruce logs on stage, one infested and one healthy (the team gets them from a forester or a sanitary-felling site).
  2. Clip the sensor on and play the infested log through the speakers. It sounds like crackling popcorn.
  3. The screen shows the event rate, "INFESTED", next to the healthy log's near-silence.
  4. Show a slide of a red-crowned stand: "By the time you can *see* it, it's too late. We hear it in week 2."
- **What the AI actually does.** It separates larval and adult feeding clicks from rain, wind, woodpeckers and handling noise, then turns the event rate into an infestation score. Fraunhofer IDMT measured about **168 acoustic events per minute in infested trunks versus 2.6 in uninfested ones**, with sensors that must be in firm contact with the wood [verified: Fraunhofer IDMT project page, via search summary]. The team's AI job is making that robust with a €10 sensor in a noisy forest, and the map of scanned trees.
- **Who uses it repeatedly, and how it scales.** State forest rangers and private forest owners would scan edge trees every 1–2 weeks through the swarming season (roughly May–August) [unverified]. The logger version gives continuous alerts. Spruce bark beetle is a Europe-wide crisis (Czechia, Germany, Sweden), and remote sensing only catches it at the "green-attack → red" stage [verified that early detection by remote sensing is hard: Forest Ecology & Management 2024 review, "Early detection of bark beetle (Ips typographus) infestations by remote sensing – a critical review"].
- **Closest existing thing.**
  - Fraunhofer IDMT's research system (lab or project stage) [verified exists].
  - Pheromone traps, some with camera counting (Trapview) [unverified].
  - Sniffer dogs used in some countries [unverified].

  **How ours differs:** it is a €30 tool in a ranger's pocket, with an open model and a shared tree map.
- **LT hook.** Spruce is a major LT tree, and bark-beetle outbreaks and the resulting "sanitary felling", including in protected areas, are a live public controversy [unverified: confirm with VMU or Valstybinių miškų urėdija news before using]. That lets the project speak to both foresters and conservationists: detect early, fell less.
- **BOM.** ESP32 (€8), a piezo or cheap accelerometer and preamp (€10), a 3D-printed spike or strap (€5), headphones (€10). Loggers add LoRa and a battery (€25 each). About **€30 handheld, €50 per logger**.
- **Biggest risk.** The season. In Sep–Nov, larvae and adults are overwintering, so live chewing is much quieter [unverified]. The team needs infested logs brought indoors, or recordings, and it's uncertain whether a €10 sensor picks up what a lab sensor does. A week-1 kill test: can we hear anything in one infested log?

### #5 Garso kamera: see sound, find the leaks (10/14; the highest WOW of the 34)

- **What it physically is.** An acoustic camera: a 3D-printed disc holding 16–32 MEMS microphones (I2S or PDM) around a Raspberry Pi camera, running delay-and-sum beamforming on a Pi 5, with sound overlaid on the live video as a heat map. For buildings, a cheap **ultrasound beacon** (a 40 kHz transducer array) sits inside the room. The camera outside shows exactly where the ultrasound escapes, which is where air, and so heat, escapes. Unlike thermal cameras, this works in September with no indoor/outdoor temperature difference.
- **Demo script.**
  1. Point the camera at the jury. When a juror whispers, a red blob sits on their mouth. This is the lean-forward moment.
  2. Bring out a window frame (a real old one from a renovation skip) with the beacon behind it. The gaps light up along the seal.
  3. Seal the gap with tape and the red spot disappears.
  4. Closing line: "Commercial acoustic cameras cost as much as a car; ours costs as much as a phone case" [unverified pricing; check before saying it].
- **What the AI actually does.**
  - Beamforming is DSP, not AI.
  - The AI layer classifies and ranks leaks: a seal gap, a frame joint, a vent, or noise and reflection.
  - It maps each hotspot onto the photo to make an auto-report: "7 leaks, 3 severe, windows 2 and 4".
  - In compressed-air mode (factories), it estimates leak size from the ultrasound level.
- **Who uses it repeatedly, and how it scales.** Renovation contractors and technical supervisors checking the airtightness of renovated blocks before handover, school caretakers, factory maintenance teams (compressed-air leaks are a well-known energy sink [unverified %]). A €200 open tool could be loaned out through municipalities or libraries.
- **Closest existing thing.**
  - Commercial acoustic imagers from Fluke, FLIR and Sorama [unverified models and prices].
  - Ultrasonic "tone generator" leak testing for doors, windows and car seals (UE Systems-style) [unverified].
  - Open-source DIY acoustic cameras on hobby sites [unverified].

  **How ours differs:** it is cheap and open, works in summer, and has an auto-report aimed at block renovation.
- **LT hook.** The mass renovation of Soviet-era blocks is under way, and residents have no easy way to check that new windows are sealed [unverified: check whether airtightness tests are mandatory for renovated A-class blocks].
- **BOM.** 16× I2S MEMS mics (€30–50), Raspberry Pi 5 (€80), camera (€25), a 3D-printed array, 40 kHz transducers and a driver (€15). About **€170–200**. A lower-cost 8-mic version runs on an ESP32 with a phone as the screen.
- **Biggest risk.**
  - Build difficulty: multi-mic synchronisation and beamforming in real time is the hardest engineering on this list.
  - Theme overlap with the covered heat-leak thermal loans. The "works without winter" framing must stay central.
  - WANT is only medium: the tool is for professionals, not something a family uses daily.

## 4. Summary
- **The strongest pattern is "we taught a cheap sensor to hear what humans can't."** It is new at school fairs, it can be played live on stage, and the AI does real signal work.
- **Best overall: Ledo ausis (Ice Ear).**
  - The physics is peer-reviewed at 5–10% accuracy, which answers "does it work?" before the jury asks.
  - It has a century-old human skill as the story, obsessive repeat users (ice fishers) and a strong LT hook.
  - Its risk is the season: no lake ice until after the concept deadline, and IceSound already exists as an academic prototype.
